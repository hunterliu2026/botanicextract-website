How to read a green tea extract specification, line by line

By Hunter Liu · BotanicExtract (Xi'an Yuanqingyuan Technology Co., Ltd.) · Published 8 November 2026 · 6 min read

What we settle before a green tea extract specification sheet goes out. Most green tea extract enquiries arrive with a competitor sheet quoting a single catechin percentage, so the first thing we establish is the assay basis: ours reads total catechins at 50% or higher with EGCG at 25% or higher as marker, both measured by HPLC, and a sheet written against a different basis cannot be compared with ours line by line. We then ask whether the formula takes the natural caffeine level or the decaffeinated option, and which application the powder is going into, before the sheet goes out. We also ask our manufacturing partner for the most recent lot COA to send alongside the sheet: the sheet states limits, the COA states results, and on a marginal lot those are two different documents.

A green tea extract specification is a short sheet, and buyers read it in the wrong order more often than in almost any other botanical category. Most people open it looking for one number, find “total catechins 50%”, and are not sure what to compare it against. This article reads the green tea extract specification in the order that matters: what the source and process lines tell you, what the assay and caffeine lines actually commit to, and what the batch COA adds on top. The commercial side — how quotations are compared and what a first order looks like — is covered in our green tea extract sourcing guide.

What the source and process lines tell you

The source line reads Camellia sinensis (L.) Kuntze, leaf. The process line reads water extraction followed by spray drying, and together they produce the yellow-green fine powder that every downstream application depends on: water-soluble, so it reconstitutes directly in ready-to-drink lines and disperses in the mix step of a beverage batch. Two details matter more than they look. First, the botanical line: Camellia sinensis leaf is the stated raw material, and that is what the “green tea” in green tea extract actually commits to. Second, the solvent line: water extraction is the process behind this grade, which is why the residual-solvent conversation is a short one — residual solvents are held within ICH Q3C limits, and the partner line dedicated to tea polyphenols keeps the material on dedicated equipment.

The assay line: what 50% catechins with 25% EGCG commits to

The assay line states total catechins at 50% or higher with EGCG at 25% or higher as marker, both measured by HPLC. That pair of lines is the single most useful filter in the category, and it is also the pair most often quoted without its basis. A sheet that reads “catechins 50%” measured one way and a sheet that reads the same number on a different basis are not the same material, so the first question to any supplier is what the percentage is measured against and whether the EGCG line is stated separately. The marker matters because a material can meet a total-catechin number while falling short on EGCG; the two lines together are what make the sheet honest. On a typical lot the COA reports 52.4% total catechins (HPLC) and 26.1% EGCG (HPLC), and alternative assays can be produced to your specification, confirmed with our manufacturing partner before quoting rather than after.

The assay line is a commitment, not a result. The sheet states what the grade commits to; the batch COA states what the lot tested.

The caffeine line and the decaf option

Caffeine is where green tea extract specifications diverge most between buyers. The standard grade retains the natural caffeine level of the extract; a typical lot carries 2.8% caffeine on the COA. Where the formulation or the destination market sets a ceiling, the decaffeinated option is a documented grade rather than a processing favour — it is a separate production decision, and it belongs on the sheet if your formula needs it. Our practical advice is to state the caffeine requirement when you request the specification, because a sheet quoted for the natural grade and a sheet quoted for the decaf grade are priced and produced differently, and comparing the two without the caffeine line is the most common way green tea extract purchases go wrong.

The physical lines: mesh, loss on drying and micro limits

Three physical lines decide whether the powder works in your process:

  • Particle size. 80 mesh is the standard grade. It blends into dry matrices without a separate milling stage and disperses in liquid mixes without a hydration step.
  • Loss on drying. Limited to 5.0% or less. This is the free-moisture line, and it is the one that matters most for flow in sachet filling and for shelf life in unopened original packing.
  • Micro and heavy-metal limits. On a typical lot the COA reports E. coli and Salmonella negative and lead below 1 ppm; the limits on your sheet should match the destination market’s requirements.

The appearance line — yellow-green fine powder — is what you check against the sample rather than against the sheet.

How we read a green tea extract specification against a quotation

When a buyer sends us a competitor sheet, we read four things in this order: the assay basis (total catechins and EGCG, both with the HPLC method stated), the caffeine line and whether a decaf grade is offered, the physical limits, and the documentation offered. Where the competitor sheet quotes a single percentage without a method, we ask for the lot COA behind it. Where the assay or caffeine line differs from our standard grade, we confirm feasibility and lead time with our manufacturing partner within one business day rather than quoting blind. OEM packing under the buyer’s own brand is drum and label work, not a change to the powder. The applications the powder is bought for — weight-management stacks, antioxidant complexes, sports and energy formulas, ready-to-drink teas and nutricosmetic blends — are covered in our green tea extract applications breakdown.

Frequently asked questions

What is green tea extract standardised to?
Total catechins at 50% or higher and EGCG at 25% or higher as marker, both measured by HPLC, from a water extraction of Camellia sinensis leaf followed by spray drying. The powder is a yellow-green fine powder at 80 mesh with loss on drying limited to 5.0% or less, and a typical lot carries 2.8% caffeine on the COA. A decaffeinated option is available as a documented grade.

What should I check when comparing green tea extract specifications?
Check the assay basis first: total catechins 50% or higher and EGCG 25% or higher, both stated with the HPLC method, because sheets written without the method cannot be compared line by line. Then the caffeine line and whether a decaf grade is offered, the mesh line (80 mesh), the loss-on-drying limit (5.0% or less), and residual solvents, which we hold within ICH Q3C limits. Ask each supplier for the lot COA that would ship with your order rather than a generic sample.

What packing and shelf life does green tea extract come in?
Standard packing is a 25 kg fibre drum with a double polyethylene liner, with 1 kg and 5 kg foil bags for samples and pilot runs. Shelf life is 24 months in unopened original packing, stored cool and dry. MOQ is 1 kg, and every lot ships with a batch COA.
