Applications

This is where creatine monohydrate applications earn their place in a formula

By Hunter Liu · BotanicExtract (Xi'an Yuanqingyuan Technology Co., Ltd.) · Published 30 October 2026 · 6 min read

The three questions we ask before recommending a creatine monohydrate application. What is the dose format — scoop, capsule, stick or shot? What does the label promise about mixability? And what is your realistic serving size? Those three answers decide the mesh, the packing and whether standard 80 mesh material is even the right starting point. Most buyers come to us with the finished product already in mind and the ingredient still unspecified, which is the right order to work in.

Four creatine monohydrate applications cover almost every enquiry we see. The material does not change between them — same fermentation-origin creatine, same 99% assay — but the mesh, the inclusion rate and the way the powder is handled in the mix do. The commercial detail is in our creatine monohydrate sourcing guide, and the sheet itself line by line in our creatine monohydrate specification breakdown.

Creatine powders and blends

This is the home application and the one that sets the category's expectations: a tub, a scoop and a 5 g serving. At 80 mesh the powder flows and fills consistently, which is what matters when your filler is running a drum an hour and cannot stop to break up bridging. Creatine monohydrate does not truly dissolve in water — it suspends — so a plain tub is the most forgiving format you can choose, and the reason so many brands launch here before moving to anything more ambitious.

Where it gets interesting is the blend. Creatine is frequently combined with protein, carbohydrate or electrolyte systems, and the practical question is always homogeneity: the creatine fraction has to be distributed evenly through a much larger mass. Specify the mesh that matches your blender's shear, and ask for a certificate from a production lot rather than a standard sample, so the material you test is the material you will receive.

Muscle-gain capsules and tablets

In capsule and tablet formats creatine is one component of a dry blend, dosed across several units rather than one scoop. Two things matter more here than in powder: flow, because the blend has to fill a capsule body or a die consistently, and density, because the serving has to fit the capsule count on your label. Fine, free-flowing material at 80 mesh is the usual answer, and compression behaviour should be trialled before you commit to a run — this is exactly what the 1 kg and 5 kg foil bags are for.

Sports energy drinks

Ready-to-drink is where creatine monohydrate applications get demanding. Because the material suspends rather than dissolves, a beverage formulator is really designing a stable suspension: particle size, viscosity and the point in the process where the creatine goes in all matter. The finer 100 mesh is usually requested here, not because it dissolves, but because a smaller particle stays in suspension longer and feels less gritty on the palate. If your label promises a clear, fully dissolved drink, creatine monohydrate is the wrong tool and we will say so before you buy it.

  • Addition point. Add with the other dry components before the final mix step; high-shear addition late in the process tends to foam.
  • Dose format. Single-serve bottles and shots mean the creatine portion has to be weighed accurately per unit, which is an argument for specifying a consistent bulk density as well as a mesh.

OEM packing is common in this category: buyers who sell under their own brand ship in their own drums and apply their own labels, with artwork handled under confidentiality.

Fitness meal replacements

In meal replacements and fitness nutrition powders the creatine is included at a defined rate alongside protein and carbohydrate, and the formulation question is usually taste masking rather than performance. Creatine monohydrate is close to neutral, which is why it disappears into a chocolate or vanilla base more easily than most actives. The practical constraint is moisture: blended powders with any hygroscopic component need the loss-on-drying line respected on both ingredients, or the creatine fraction will start converting to creatinine on the shelf.

Choosing the mesh for your creatine monohydrate application

Across all four applications the mesh decision follows the same logic. Choose 80 mesh for scoop powders, capsules, tablets and meal replacements — it flows, fills and costs less. Choose 100 mesh for ready-to-drink, stick packs and anything where the customer will notice texture. What should not change between them is documentation: whichever mesh you specify, the COA has to be issued for the lot that ships, and the assay has to be stated per lot rather than as a typical figure.

Frequently asked questions

What applications is creatine monohydrate used for?
The product page lists four: creatine powders and blends, muscle-gain capsules and tablets, sports energy drinks, and fitness meal replacements. The material is the same in all four at 99% assay; what changes is the mesh — 80 mesh as standard, 100 mesh on request for drink formats and stick packs.

Which mesh size should I choose for my creatine monohydrate application?
80 mesh is the standard grade and suits all four application areas, particularly scoop powders, capsules and meal replacements. Specify 100 mesh on request when the format is a ready-to-drink beverage or a single-serve stick pack, where suspension stability and mouthfeel matter more.

How do I run a formula trial with creatine monohydrate?
Ask for a 1 kg or 5 kg foil bag drawn from a production lot, so what you trial is what you will receive in bulk. Check flow and fill on your own equipment first, then compression or suspension behaviour depending on the format, and confirm the mesh before you place the production order.
